1. Introduction and Motivation
Specific Learning Disorder (SLD) is among the most prevalent yet least identified conditions affecting Indian schoolchildren. The Ernakulam Learning Disorder (ELD) study, a rigorous school-based prevalence study in Kerala using the NIMHANS Index and Malin's Intelligence Scale, found an SLD prevalence of 16.49% among children in standards 4–7, with impairment in written expression (15.6%) being the most common subtype, ahead of reading (12.57%) and mathematics (9.93%). Its most consequential finding for this research: none of the 244 children identified with SLD had ever been evaluated or received remedial education prior to the study. The barrier is not awareness alone but arithmetic — a small number of specialists cannot administer hour-long subjective assessments to lakhs of children, and no scalable early-identification mechanism exists in Indian schools.
Early identification is where the value lies. Intervention research consistently shows that structured, phonics-based support delivered at the preschool and early-primary stage measurably closes literacy gaps even in children at significant risk, whereas late-identified children carry the gap forward. A system that notices difficulties early, specifies them precisely, and tracks whether help is working is therefore of direct educational and public-health significance.
Handwriting is the ideal signal source for such a system — as a sensor, not as the disorder itself. It is produced daily in every classroom, requires no equipment beyond a phone camera, and, crucially, a child's letters may be cleanly formed while what is written is linguistically wrong: the handwritten word jointly encodes orthographic errors (vowel signs, conjuncts, spelling) and, through the spelling pattern, the child's underlying phonological representation. This research therefore targets language difficulties (the dyslexia-type profile) revealed through handwriting, rather than motoric writing difficulty (dysgraphia). The 2025 University at Buffalo DDBIC framework established that the majority of behavioral indicators of dyslexia and dysgraphia (reversals, spelling errors, transpositions) are extractable from static handwriting images.
This research targets Malayalam, the scholar's native language and the literacy medium of millions of Kerala children, spoken by roughly 45 million people. Two properties make Malayalam both necessary and advantageous. Necessary: a screening instrument must operate in the child's medium of literacy instruction, and state-syllabus children — the group at significantly higher SLD risk (odds ratio 6.97 in the ELD study) — learn in Malayalam. Advantageous: Malayalam is a near-transparent akshara (alpha-syllabic) orthography in which spelling errors map to phonological causes far more directly than in deep English orthography, making error analysis more diagnostic. English is explicitly excluded as a target language, both because it is already saturated with research and because the architecture exploits alphasyllabic structure that transfers to other Indic scripts (Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, Devanagari) rather than to English.
2. Problem Statement
Language difficulties involving orthographic and phonological processing are the most prevalent yet least screened form of SLD among Indian primary-school children, and they are visible in everyday handwritten output: the writing act itself may be fluent, but what is written is linguistically wrong — phoneme–grapheme mismappings, vowel-length errors, retroflex confusions. Here handwriting is the sensor, not the disorder; the target is the language difficulty revealed through it (the dyslexia-type profile), distinct from motoric writing difficulty (dysgraphia), which is outside the primary scope. Early identification is decisive, yet screening today depends on scarce specialists administering subjective, time-intensive assessments, and no such system exists for Malayalam. Existing computational approaches that analyse handwriting (a) overwhelmingly target English/Latin scripts and adult handwriting; (b) treat recognition accuracy as the goal and therefore auto-correct away the very errors that carry the diagnostic signal — our own benchmarking shows frontier vision-language models such as GPT-5.1 and Claude Opus 4 achieve only 20–21% character accuracy on children's Malayalam handwriting precisely because they normalize non-standard spellings; and (c) stop at one-time detection — a binary label — offering neither an explanation of which specific difficulties the child shows and what to work on, nor any mechanism to track whether the child is improving, stagnant, or worsening over time.
This research proposes an end-to-end identification–insight–monitoring framework in which error-preserving handwritten character recognition and error-preserving grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) analysis of Malayalam children's writing feed an agentic diagnostic layer that maintains a longitudinal, per-error-class learner model; every new writing sample is compared against the child's own history to produce actionable insights and progress verdicts; and an agentic intervention layer converts those insights into personalized, tiered remedial activities whose effects are measured by the same error-preserving perception pipeline — a closed loop under teacher supervision, with an architecture designed to transfer to other akshara-based scripts.
The system's identity is monitoring-and-insight, not one-shot detection: it answers not "does this child have a problem?" but three continuous questions — which difficulties does this child show, what should we work on, and is the help working? The system is a screening and support tool, not a diagnostic instrument; final diagnosis remains with clinicians.
3. Scope and Definitions
Target population: children in the early literacy-acquisition band (approximately ages 4–8, preschool through standard 2–3), where intervention effectiveness is strongest.
Unit of analysis: offline handwriting (scanned or photographed paper). Tablet-based online features (pressure, speed, pauses) are identified as future work.
Language scope: Malayalam as the primary target; cross-lingual transfer to at least one additional akshara-script language as the generalization claim. English is out of scope.
Measurement mode: structured probe tasks (dictation and picture-naming with known target words), mirroring established Response-to-Intervention (RTI) progress-monitoring practice, so that every diagnostic claim is alignment-verified. Extension to unconstrained free writing is identified as future work.
Role boundary: the system provides screening, insight, and progress monitoring for teachers and special educators; it does not diagnose. Human-in-the-loop oversight is a design requirement throughout.
4. Literature Review and Research Gap
The thesis sits at the intersection of four research streams. The table summarizes representative work, what each achieves, and the specific gap this research occupies.
The gap statement follows directly: no existing work connects error-preserving recognition, linguistically-interpreted error profiling, and agent-delivered tiered monitored intervention, in any language — and nothing close exists for Malayalam or any akshara script. The scholar's two under-review works close the first two links (perception and phonological interpretation); the doctoral contribution is the third link — the agentic diagnostic-and-intervention layer.
4.1 Why error preservation is the core principle
Standard handwriting-recognition research optimizes accuracy against a canonical label, treating a child's mistake as noise to be corrected. For this application the error is the data. The scholar's HERA work operationalizes this at the visual layer — retrieval-only recognition with no decoder, so the retrieved label is the output and cannot be normalized by a language prior — and the OSUR/G2P work does the same at the phonological layer, transcribing mis-spelled akshara input without correction. Clinical literature adds the second half of the argument: the pattern of errors differentiates conditions — children with dysgraphia produce near-correct spellings (intact phonology, weak orthographic memory), while children with dyslexia make phonologically-deviant spellings reflecting phonological processing deficits.
5. Research Questions and Objectives
5.1 Research questions
RQ1 (Recognition) — answered by HERA (under review).
Can offline Malayalam handwritten word recognition be formulated as error-preserving retrieval, preserving children's spelling deviations with fidelity sufficient for downstream linguistic analysis? Answer: yes — retrieval-only recognition achieves CER 0.17–0.20 on unseen children's writing and outperforms generative VLMs and LLMs that normalize non-standard spellings.
RQ2 (Phonological inference) — answered by OSUR-G2P (under review).
Given an error-preserved recognized word, can the child's encoded phonological form and the type of deviation (orthographic vs phonological) be recovered automatically? Answer: yes — OSUR akshara tokenization with per-step decoder-conditioned alignment reaches PER 0.84% / GUPER 2.82%, transcribing mis-spelled input that rule-based tools reject.
RQ3 (Agentic identification & insight) — open; the doctoral core.
Can a tool-augmented agentic layer, holding a longitudinal learner memory built from HERA+G2P snapshots, identify which specific orthographic and phonological difficulties a child shows and generate teacher-actionable, evidence-traced insights — with tool-mediated agents producing more faithful insights than free-reasoning LLM agents, and at-risk identification validated against a standard instrument (NIMHANS Index–based assessment)?
RQ3b (Agentic progress monitoring) — open.
Can the Insight Agent, reasoning over within-child temporal error trajectories via deterministic statistical tools, reliably classify each monitored difficulty as improving / stagnant / worsening, with verdicts matching measured learning gains and giving earlier signal than periodic re-screening or teacher observation alone?
RQ4 (Agentic intervention) — open.
Can the Intervention Planner and Content Agent translate a difficulty profile and its progress verdicts into a personalized, RTI/MTSS-consistent plan with phonology-constrained Malayalam content — escalating or de-escalating tiers when a difficulty stagnates or resolves — outperforming a static baseline on learning-gain metrics?
RQ5 (Generalization) — open.
Do the error schema, the error-preserving methodology, and the agent orchestration transfer to at least one additional akshara-script language with only script-level (not architecture-level) changes?
5.2 Objectives
O1. Extend the scholar's 12-rule error taxonomy into a full diagnostic schema spanning orthographic, phonological, phono-orthographic, motoric, and sequencing classes.
O2. Design and implement a longitudinal learner memory — a structured, per-child, per-error-class store of handwriting-derived evidence.
O3. Build the agentic layer: an Error-Analysis Agent, a self-verifying Insight Agent, an Intervention Planner and Content Agent, and an Orchestrator with a teacher gate.
O4. Validate at-risk identification against a standard instrument and validate progress verdicts against measured learning gains.
O5. Demonstrate cross-script transfer to at least one additional akshara-script language.
6. Work Completed (Under Review)
Two foundational works are completed and under peer review. Together they form the perception layer of the framework and provide the empirical basis for its central design decisions.
Two further assets support the doctoral work: a linguist-labeled dataset of approximately 1,000 error instances classified through Haridas's Malayalam spelling-acquisition framework at the orthographic and phonological level (used as the Error-Analysis Agent's knowledge base and the gold standard for classification evaluation), and institutional ethics approval (AMRITNSOE/ADMN/DON03/2026/002) already in place for child-data collection.
These works establish the central design finding of the research: error preservation must be architectural, not prompted. Because frontier LLMs destroy the diagnostic signal at perception and rule-based tools reject learner spellings at phonology, the diagnostic evidence chain cannot itself be an LLM — which is precisely why the doctoral agentic layer is designed to orchestrate deterministic tools rather than replace them.
7. Proposed Methodology: The Agentic Diagnostic-Intervention Layer
7.1 Architecture overview
The framework has three pillars. P1 (HERA) sees: it transcribes the child's handwriting verbatim, errors intact, each character traceable to a retrieved neighbourhood. P2 (OSUR-G2P) hears: it tokenizes the literal sequence into akshara units and transcribes it to IPA without correction, classifying each error against the taxonomy. P3 — the doctoral contribution — understands and acts: an agentic layer holds a longitudinal learner memory and, on every new sample, classifies errors, updates the child's difficulty profile and trajectory, generates insights and progress verdicts, and plans and generates RTI-aligned intervention, then re-assesses through P1 and P2 to close the loop.
7.2 The diagnostic error schema
The scholar's existing 12-rule taxonomy (six orthographic, four phonological, two phono-orthographic rules, derived from 3,800 authentic learner spellings) is promoted from an augmentation device into a diagnostic classification schema and extended with motoric and sequencing classes observable from HERA's visual output (letter misformation, spacing, reversals, conjunct simplification, transpositions, abandoned words). The clinical anchor for the split: dysgraphia-type children produce near-correct spellings (intact phonology, weak orthographic memory) while dyslexia-type children produce phonologically-deviant spellings.
7.3 The agentic layer
The layer is a tool-augmented, memory-holding, self-verifying multi-agent system. Agents reason and act; verified components see, measure, and remember. The constituent agents are summarized below.
7.4 Longitudinal monitoring and probe mode
Every new writing sample produces a per-error-class snapshot (error rate with uncertainty, mastery estimate) appended to the child's memory. Because the diagnostic pipeline is alignment-based, measurement adopts probe mode: short, structured tasks (10–15 grade-leveled words by dictation or picture-naming) administered every 2–3 weeks, in which the target words are known by construction. Probe difficulty is held constant within a tracking period, and the Content Agent that generates practice material also generates the next probe, so the intended word is never missing inside the loop. Verdicts are issued only when statistically supported (e.g., five to six spaced points for a defensible trend).

The claim is akshara-script transfer, not universal coverage, structured as a protocol: swap the character inventory and rebuild the retrieval index (P1); swap the G2P and syllabification engine (P2 — OSUR and GUPER are unit-based, not Malayalam-specific); keep P3 unchanged except for language-specific error subtypes. 11. Risks, Ethics, and Mitigation
Data scarcity — the field-wide bottleneck is children's handwriting data. Mitigation: school partnerships established early, rule-based synthetic error augmentation (already demonstrated in the G2P work), and the existing public character knowledge base.
Label quality — at-risk labels require a defensible source. Mitigation: a two-stage design (teacher screening plus NIMHANS-index-style clinician confirmation on a subsample), mirroring the ELD study; error-type gold labels come from the linguist-labeled dataset.
Ethics and overclaiming — the system screens and supports, never diagnoses; the teacher remains in the loop; child-data consent follows the existing institutional approval (AMRITNSOE/ADMN/DON03/2026/002) with guardian consent and age-appropriate assent.
LLM reliability — agents do not perceive or compute trends; they orchestrate deterministic tools, verify claims against raw data, and defer to the teacher gate, preserving traceability.
